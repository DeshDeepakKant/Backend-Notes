### 1. Behavioral interviews matter a LOT

* Behavioral interviews are a major signal in **hiring + leveling**, especially for senior engineers.
* Don't treat them as something you can "wing."
* Interviewers are evaluating:

  * Scope
  * Influence
  * Communication
  * Organization
  * Technical/business impact
  * Leadership

### 2. You're being evaluated from the first recruiter call

* The recruiter/sourcer call is **not just a casual conversation**.
* They use it to form an initial view of your level.
* Be prepared with a strong **"Tell me about yourself"**.
* Clearly communicate:

  * What you've built
  * Scale of problems
  * Your impact
  * Complexity
  * Measurable results

### 3. Level ≠ just years of experience

Companies may initially use:

* Years of experience
* Previous title
* Previous company

But they ultimately assess whether your **actual experience matches the expected scope**.

Different companies can have different level expectations, so titles don't automatically transfer.

### 4. Scope is the biggest factor

When choosing behavioral stories:

**Scope > Relevance > Recency**

A strong story demonstrates:

* Large business/technical problem
* High ambiguity
* Multiple teams/stakeholders
* Significant organizational influence
* Measurable impact

### 5. Understand the level hierarchy

Think of engineering work roughly as:

**Task → Feature → Project → Goal → Organization → Business/Industry**

* Junior/New Grad → individual tasks
* Mid-level → features
* Senior → projects, often through/with other engineers
* Staff → organizational goals involving multiple projects/teams
* Principal+ → organization/business-wide impact

The underlying idea: **higher levels handle greater ambiguity and broader scope.** 

### 6. For senior/staff interviews, don't talk only about technology

Show the **non-technical work** too:

* Influencing other teams
* Mentoring
* Delegation
* Resolving conflicts
* Aligning stakeholders
* Roadmap influence
* Managing up
* Improving the organization

### 7. Quantify complexity

Don't simply say:

> "I improved performance."

Instead explain:

* How many users/engineers/teams?
* How long did it take?
* What was the technical difficulty?
* What was the risk?
* How many stakeholders?
* What was the business impact?

**Make complexity visible.**

### 8. Behavioral questions have a "question behind the question"

If asked:

> "Tell me about a conflict."

They're not necessarily interested in the conflict itself.

They're trying to determine:

**"Does this person operate at the level we're hiring for?"**

So choose stories that expose your strongest evidence of the required level. 

### 9. Use the Decode → Select → Deliver framework

**Decode**

* What signal is the interviewer looking for?

**Select**

* Which story demonstrates that signal **with the highest scope/impact**?

**Deliver**

* Tell the story clearly and directly.

Don't blindly follow a rehearsed story if it doesn't answer the signal being tested.

### 10. Build a small set of "core stories"

You don't need a completely different story for every question.

One strong project can demonstrate:

* Ambiguity
* Conflict
* Leadership
* Communication
* Technical complexity
* Impact

Think of it like creating different **"trailers" for the same movie** depending on the question.

### 11. Know the company's behavioral rubric

Every company may assess different signals.

Example from the discussion for Meta:

* Driving results
* Embracing ambiguity
* Resolving conflicts
* Growing continuously
* Communicating effectively

For smaller companies, ask the recruiter/team:

> "What makes engineers successful here?"

Then prepare stories around those signals. 

### 12. Company-specific preparation

**OpenAI**

* "Act with humility" → growth/feedback mindset
* "Feel the AGI" → optimism and enthusiasm around AI's potential

**Anthropic**

* "Hold light and shade" → understand **both positive and negative implications of AI**
* When discussing AI projects, show that you considered risks and mitigations.

### 13. Common behavioral mistakes

**❌ Choosing low-scope stories**

* Biggest mistake.

**❌ Too much context**

* Don't spend minutes explaining background.
* Get to **what YOU did**.

**❌ Too much technical detail**

* Technical depth matters, but don't bury the behavioral signal.

**❌ Not discussing people**

* Senior candidates need to show influence, mentoring, alignment, etc.

**❌ Long, unstructured stories**

* Organize the story into clear sections.

**❌ Giving stories that can be interpreted negatively**

* Think about how an interviewer might interpret your actions.
* Provide relevant context and reasoning.

### 14. Organize long stories with a "table of contents"

For a large project, start with something like:

1. Business context
2. How I initiated it
3. Stakeholder alignment
4. Difficult technical problems
5. Rollout
6. Results

This keeps long stories structured and makes it easier to handle interruptions/follow-ups.

### 15. Referrals help most when you're borderline

According to the interview:

* Strong referrals can matter significantly.
* A referral from someone who **actually worked with you** carries more useful signal than a generic referral.
* Referrals were described as particularly helpful when a candidate is **on the bubble**, such as getting a follow-up interview. 
* They don't replace interview performance when there's a clear consensus that the candidate doesn't meet the bar.

### 16. Compensation

* Avoid **early anchoring** on compensation expectations.
* Multiple offers can improve your negotiating position.
* Know the applicable salary-transparency laws for your location.

### 17. Biggest takeaway

> **Don't just have impressive experience — learn how to communicate the scope, complexity, leadership, and impact of that experience.**

For interviews, your goal is to make the interviewer clearly see **the level at which you actually operate**.
