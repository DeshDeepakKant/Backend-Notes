## Behavioral Interview Notes — Part 2

### 1. Pivot to stronger stories

* Answer the question, but **guide the interviewer toward a higher-scope story** when appropriate.
* Example: If asked about conflict with a PM, mention it briefly, then ask if you can discuss a larger conflict involving more stakeholders.
* Goal: expose the **strongest signal**, not merely answer literally. 

### 2. Don't talk for too long

* Different interviewers have different styles:

  * Some let you speak freely.
  * Some interrupt frequently.
* Don't assume interruptions mean you're failing.
* Watch for engagement: eye contact, note-taking, follow-ups.
* If unsure, ask: **"Is this what you're looking for?"**
* Avoid long monologues; **2–4 minutes** is a useful upper guideline for an answer. 

### 3. Prep differently by level

| Level         | Main focus                                                                                      |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Junior**    | Coding/technical skills are most important; behavioral shows thinking, motivation, potential    |
| **Mid-level** | Balance coding + system design + behavioral; ownership and ability to learn matter              |
| **Senior**    | Behavioral becomes much more important                                                          |
| **Staff+**    | Behavioral, organizational impact, influence, scope and leadership become major differentiators |

* At senior/staff level, technical interviews often become more of a **bar you must clear**, while behavioral evidence differentiates level. 

### 4. Start behavioral preparation early

Before interviews:

* Identify your **core career stories**
* Study company values
* Find the behavioral rubric
* Map stories → signal areas
* Practice with mock interviews
* For major companies, ideally get feedback from someone familiar with that company's interview calibration. 

### 5. Senior candidates are judged on potential organizational impact

At higher levels, interviewers look at:

* Cross-functional collaboration
* Organizational influence
* Conflict resolution
* Scope
* Business impact
* Project depth
* Leadership

Additional interviews may include:

* Cross-functional/PM interview
* Project deep dive
* Large-project discussion. 

### 6. First few minutes matter

* The beginning of the interview can strongly shape the interviewer's impression.
* Lead with your **highest-scope, highest-impact stories**.
* Weak opening stories can make it harder to recover later.
* A strong **"Tell me about yourself"** is extremely important. 

### 7. Match the interviewer's style

* Business-like interviewer → be concise and structured.
* Conversational interviewer → build some rapport.
* Don't force a bubbly personality.
* **Match their energy while remaining professional.**
* Calmness and confidence become more important at senior levels. 

### 8. Your presence matters more at senior levels

At senior levels, they're also observing whether you can:

* Stay calm under pressure
* Communicate confidently
* Build relationships
* Discuss difficult topics clearly
* Present yourself well in high-pressure meetings

But **substance still matters more than presentation**. 

### 9. "Do you have any questions?" is also evaluative

Ask questions that reveal what **you actually care about**.

Good categories:

* Role success
* Team
* Manager
* Career growth
* Product/company
* Technical direction

Good example:

> **"What makes someone really successful in this role?"**

Avoid generic questions like:

* "What's a day in the life?"
* "What's the free food like?"
* Questions that could have been answered by basic research. 

### 10. Storytelling framework: CALR

**C — Context**
What's happening? Why does the problem exist?

**A — Actions**
What **did YOU do**?

**L/R — Results**
What changed because of your work?

**L — Learnings**
What did you learn / what would you do differently?

The speaker prefers **CALR (Context → Actions → Results → Learnings)** over traditional STAR, especially for senior engineers, because it includes **reflection and judgment**. 

### 11. Understand question depth

**Trunk question** → broad question → give a full story.

**Branch question** → asks about one part → give focused detail.

**Leaf question** → asks for a specific fact → answer directly and move on.

A senior candidate should understand **how much detail the interviewer actually wants** rather than giving the same long answer every time. 

### 12. Substance > delivery

* The most important thing is **what you say**.
* Your story must actually demonstrate the required signal.
* At senior levels, **how you communicate it** becomes increasingly important too.
* Include details only when they demonstrate something useful:

  * Technical depth
  * Influence
  * Conflict handling
  * Judgment
  * Leadership

### 13. Career growth: CREATE scope

One of the strongest career lessons:

> **Don't wait for someone to give you the scope required for the next level. Create it.**

To advance:

* Take on bigger problems
* Expand your impact beyond your immediate team
* Create opportunities for the organization to benefit
* Work backward from what leadership should eventually be saying about your work. 

### 14. Biggest overall takeaway

**For higher-level engineering interviews:**

> **Technical ability gets you over the bar.
> Scope + impact + leadership + communication determine how you're perceived.**

And the same principle applies to career growth: **operate at the scope of the level you want before expecting the title.**
